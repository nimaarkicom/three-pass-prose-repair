Mara hadn't been back to the lighthouse in six years. The paint was worse than she remembered, flaking off the north wall in long strips, and someone had propped a bent bicycle against the door like it belonged there.

Samuel didn't look up when she knocked. "Took you long enough," he said, and kept sanding a plank he'd had clamped to the table since, as far as she could tell, the Clinton administration.

She sat across from him without being asked. Outside, a trawler she didn't recognize was working the near bank, engine coughing every few seconds like it needed a mechanic more than a captain did.

"Dad's selling the house," she said.

Samuel didn't stop sanding. "Figured he would eventually."

That was it. No speech about roots, no line about how a place gets into your blood. He just kept working the plank, and after a while she picked up the second sander off the shelf where it had always sat and started on the other end of it, because it was easier than talking and because some part of her missed the rhythm of it, the plain scrape of grit against wood.

They didn't finish the plank that day. Samuel's hands weren't what they used to be, and he had to stop twice to shake them out, muttering something she didn't quite catch. She almost asked if he was okay. She didn't.

Later, walking back along the shingle, she passed the spot where she'd broken her arm at nine, and felt nothing in particular about it, which surprised her more than feeling something would have.

She wasn't sure yet if she was staying. Her father wanted the house sold by spring. The lighthouse job, if Samuel would even let her near it, paid less than she made answering emails in a city three hours south. None of that resolved itself on the walk home, and she didn't expect it to.
